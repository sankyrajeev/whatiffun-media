What if an iron bar shot straight through your head? September eighteen forty-eight, Vermont. Phineas Gage, twenty-five, is blasting rock for a new railroad. He packs gunpowder into a hole with an iron rod about a metre long. Six kilos. A spark sets it off. The rod rockets up through his left cheek, behind his eye, and out the top of his skull. It lands twenty-five metres away. And Phineas? He's awake. Within minutes, he's talking. He survives. But his friends say he has changed. Rude, restless, swearing. They say he's no longer Gage. His case helps scientists link the front of the brain to who we are. Phineas moves to Chile and drives stagecoaches for about seven years. He lives twelve more years after the accident. Today his skull and the rod sit side by side in a Harvard museum. Subscribe for more weird humans.
